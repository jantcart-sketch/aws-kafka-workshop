The Core Difference / 02.1

A queue you drain.
A log you read.

In classic messaging the broker's job is delivery: hold the message until a consumer acknowledges it, then forget it. The broker owns the state of "who got what". In event streaming the broker's job is storage: append the record to an ordered log, keep it for a retention period, and let every reader track its own position.

click the button to switch models
The one sentence

Consumption is not destruction

Reading a record does not remove it. A Kafka consumer moves a cursor; it does not take the message off the broker. Records leave only when the retention policy says so — by age, by size, or by being superseded on the same key.

So who holds the state?

In a queue, the broker tracks per-message delivery state. In a log, the consumer group tracks a single number per partition: its committed offset. That shift is why one topic can feed six independent teams at different speeds — and why "please resend last Tuesday" stops being a project.

◆
Kafka also has an ordinary pub/sub face — produce, subscribe, consume. The log is what it keeps underneath, and that's the part worth understanding today.
Why It Exists / 02.2

The problem: N×M integrations.

This is the shape most integration estates are actually in. Every new system that needs data adds another bespoke pipeline — with 6 sources and 6 sinks you don't have 12 connections to maintain, you have 36, each with its own format, retry logic, backpressure story and 3am pager.

before → after · click to toggle
What actually changes

From O(N×M) to O(N+M)

Each producer learns one protocol. Each consumer learns one protocol. Adding the seventh consumer costs one subscription, not six integrations — and costs the producer team nothing at all.

The real win is decoupling in time

The producer doesn't need the consumer to exist yet. A team can start consuming a stream that has been running for six months — and read all of it. That is impossible with a queue that deletes on delivery, or an RPC call that requires both sides to be up.

◆
This was LinkedIn's original 2010 problem statement: dozens of data systems, each pair wired by hand. Kafka was written to be the single central nervous system — the design follows from that goal, not from "we needed a faster queue".

The Payoff / 02.4

What the retained log
actually buys you.

Four consequences follow from "the record stays" — and one honest cost. Each one maps to a pain somebody named in Chapter 01.

one log · five consumer groups · one copy of the data

01Fan-out without coordination

One orders topic feeds fulfilment, fraud, analytics and the lake. Adding the fifth consumer needs no change to the producer and doesn't slow the other four.

02Replay & reprocessing

Bug in a rule? Reset that group to yesterday 09:00 and re-run. New feature? Backfill from the start of retention. The data is still there, in order.

03Temporal decoupling

Consumers can be down for hours and catch up. Producers never block on a slow consumer, and the consumer never loses data — it just has lag you can alarm on.

—And the honest costs

You own retention and storage sizing, partition-count decisions, schema discipline, and the loss of per-message niceties.

◆
Two more that follow: with compaction a topic becomes a rebuildable table (latest value per key — you'll build one in Lab 2), and because history is addressable you can run continuous SQL or Java over live and historical data with one engine.
✓
The line to remember: a queue moves work between two systems; a log publishes facts that any number of systems can interpret, now or later. Most "integration projects" are really the second thing wearing the first thing's clothes.

Things that are simply not there
  • No native DLQ. You write failures to a *.dlt topic yourself — or your framework does (Spring Kafka, Flink, Lambda ESM with on-failure destination).
  • No per-message priority. Separate topics per priority class is the usual answer.
  • No delayed delivery. Schedule outside the broker, or re-drive via a delay topic.
  • No server-side selectors/filters. Consumers read everything in their partitions and filter — or you split topics.
  • No per-message visibility timeout or redelivery count. A slow consumer blocks its partition; you handle retries in your code.
  • No message browsing/deletion by ID. You can read a specific offset, not pluck one message out.
▲
This list is the most common source of disappointment in migrations. If a flow depends on three of these, that flow probably wants to stay on a queue.
Structure / 03.2

Topics, partitions, keys.

A topic is split into partitions — independent append-only logs. The partition is the unit of ordering, of parallelism, and of replication. The producer picks a partition from the record's key: same key → same partition → guaranteed order for that key.

key → hash → partition · order preserved per key
Three rules worth memorising
  • Ordering is per partition only. There is no global order across a topic — and asking for one means one partition, i.e. no parallelism.
  • The key decides the partition. Default is hash(key) mod partitions; null key spreads records round-robin-ish across partitions.
  • Choose the key for the ordering you need. customer_id if per-customer order matters; order_id if per-order does. This is the single most consequential design decision on a topic.
✕
Adding partitions later breaks the mapping. hash(key) mod N changes when N changes, so an existing key can land in a new partition and its old and new records sit in two logs. Size partitions with growth in mind; over-provisioning a little is cheap, re-keying later is not.
◆
Hot keys are real. If 40% of your traffic is one customer_id, one partition carries 40% of the load. Watch for it in your own key choice.

Reading / 03.4

Consumer groups —
competing consumers, with a cursor.

A consumer group is the subscription. Kafka assigns each partition to exactly one consumer in the group, so adding processes scales you out — until you run out of partitions. Different groups are completely independent: same records, separate positions.

6 partitions · add consumers and watch the assignment
What to notice
  • Partitions cap your parallelism. 6 partitions, 7 consumers → one consumer sits idle. Forever. This surprises people in production.
  • Rebalance. Join, leave or crash and the group reassigns partitions — brief pause for the affected members. Cooperative/incremental rebalancing keeps most consumers working.
  • Lag is your SLO. lag = log end offset − committed offset, per partition. It's the number you alarm on.
  • Commits are positional. Committing offset 105 means "everything below 105 is done". Commit after processing, or you've built at-most-once by accident.
✕
One slow record blocks its partition. There is no per-message visibility timeout to rescue you. Long or unreliable work belongs behind the stream — read the record, hand the job to a queue or a worker pool, commit.
Durability / 03.5

Replication, ISR,
and losing a broker.

Each partition has a leader and followers on other brokers. Clients only talk to the leader; followers replicate. The set that's caught up is the ISR (in-sync replicas). Kill the leader and a caught-up follower is elected — clients re-discover and carry on.

replication.factor=3 · min.insync.replicas=2
The configuration that makes it true
  • replication.factor=3, spread across three Availability Zones — the default shape on AWS.
  • min.insync.replicas=2 — a write with acks=all needs two copies. Survive one broker/AZ loss with zero data loss and zero write downtime.
  • Unclean leader election disabled (the default) — a stale replica is never promoted, so you never silently lose committed records.
  • Consumers read committed data up to the high-water mark — you can't read a record that isn't replicated.
◆
Modern Kafka uses KRaft for cluster metadata — the controller quorum replaced ZooKeeper (removed entirely in Kafka 4.0). One less system to operate; on MSK you don't see it at all.
✓
Demo moment: press the button. Note that the client needs no reconfiguration — leader election plus metadata refresh is the whole failover story.
Guarantees / 03.6

At-least-once is the default.
Plan for duplicates.

The guarantee you get depends on when you commit relative to when you act. Nearly every production pipeline is at-least-once plus idempotent consumers — and that is a perfectly respectable answer.

At-most-once

COMMIT, THEN PROCESS

Crash after the commit and the record is never processed. Almost never what you want — and the accidental result of enable.auto.commit=true with slow processing.

At-least-once

PROCESS, THEN COMMIT · DEFAULT

Crash before the commit and you reprocess from the last commit. Duplicates are possible, loss is not. Make your consumer idempotent — upsert by key, dedupe on an event ID — and you're done.

Exactly-once (EOS)

TRANSACTIONS · KAFKA→KAFKA

Idempotent producer + transactions commit records and offsets atomically; consumers set isolation.level=read_committed. Real, but scoped to Kafka-to-Kafka processing — the moment you write to an external system, it's your idempotency again.

The pattern that matters most in practice
// at-least-once with an idempotent write
while (running) {
  records = consumer.poll(200ms);
  for (r : records)
      repo.upsert(r.key(), r.value());   // idempotent by key
  consumer.commitSync();                 // commit AFTER the work
}
// enable.auto.commit=false
Rules of thumb
  • Turn off auto-commit for anything you care about, and commit after the side effect.
  • Design for duplicates from day one — natural keys, upserts, or a dedupe store with a TTL.
  • Reach for transactions only for Kafka→Kafka stream processing; measure the latency cost.
  • Don't confuse ordering with exactly-once. Per-partition ordering is free; de-duplication is not.
How Long It Lives / 03.7

Retention, and the
topic that is a table.

Retention replaces per-message TTL, and it comes in two flavours. The second one — compaction — is how a topic becomes a durable, rebuildable view of current state rather than a window of recent history.

run the cleaner · watch what survives
cleanup.policy=delete — a window of history

Segments are discarded past retention.ms (default 7 days) or retention.bytes. Set it from the longest replay you want. Consumers that fall behind retention lose data — so alarm on lag in time, not just records. On MSK, tiered storage keeps long retention cheap.

cleanup.policy=compact — latest value per key

Kafka keeps at least the most recent record for every key, forever. A null value is a tombstone — it deletes the key. Rebuild a cache or a whole service's state by reading from 0; the backbone of event sourcing, CQRS read models and reference-data distribution.

retention.ms=604800000     # 7 days
cleanup.policy=delete       # or compact, or "compact,delete"
max.message.bytes=1048576  # 1 MiB default
▲
Large payloads: Kafka is not a file transfer system. Put blobs in S3 and publish the pointer plus metadata as the record — the claim-check pattern. Multi-MB records wreck batching, replication and memory sizing.
Contracts / 03.8

The schema is the API.

In messaging, a payload change is a conversation with the one consumer you know about. With a retained log read by many groups — including replays of old records by new code — the payload format becomes a long-lived public contract. This is the discipline that decides whether your platform ages well.

Pick a format

  • Avro — compact, schema-first, strong evolution rules. The default choice with a registry.
  • Protobuf — compact, great tooling, familiar to service teams.
  • JSON Schema — human-readable, larger on the wire, easiest adoption.

Register it

  • AWS Glue Schema Registry — serverless, IAM-governed, native MSK/Flink/Lambda integration.
  • Producers register and embed a schema ID; consumers resolve it — the full schema isn't on every record.
  • Set a compatibility mode and let CI enforce it.

Evolve it safely

  • BACKWARD — new schema reads old data. Add optional fields with defaults; never remove a required one.
  • FORWARD — old consumers read new data.
  • FULL — both. The right default for a shared platform topic.
Backward-compatible change — add a field with a default
{ "type": "record", "name": "Order",
  "fields": [
    { "name": "orderId",  "type": "string" },
    { "name": "amount",   "type": "double" },
    { "name": "channel",  "type": "string",
      "default": "unknown" }   // ← safe addition
  ] }
✕
Breaking changes — renaming a field, narrowing a type, removing a required field, or changing semantics while keeping the name. The last one is the dangerous one: the registry can't catch it, and every replay of old data will be wrong.
✓
Do this from the first topic. Retrofitting schema governance onto twenty live topics with unknown consumers is the most expensive avoidable lesson in this space.
On AWS / 03.9

Kafka on AWS —
what you actually run.

Amazon MSK runs open-source Apache Kafka: same protocol, same clients, same topic configs, no code lock-in. What AWS takes over is brokers, patching, replication across AZs, metadata, monitoring and recovery. Choose the flavour by how much control you need.

MSK Provisioned

YOU PICK THE BROKERS

Choose instance type and count; control broker and topic configuration. Express brokers give you much higher throughput per broker and faster recovery. Tiered storage for long retention. The default for serious production workloads.

MSK Serverless

NO CAPACITY DECISIONS

No brokers to size, throughput scales automatically, pay for what you use. IAM authentication only. Excellent for workshops, dev/test, spiky or unknown workloads — check the current per-cluster quotas before you commit production to it.

Self-managed on EC2/EKS

TOTAL CONTROL, TOTAL OWNERSHIP

You run brokers, upgrades, rebalancing and recovery. Choose it when you need a specific distribution or feature AWS doesn't expose yet — and you have the team to operate it.

The ecosystem you get around it
  • MSK Connect — managed Kafka Connect for source/sink connectors (CDC in, S3/OpenSearch out) without running Connect workers.
  • MSK Replicator — cross-region/cross-cluster replication for DR and migration.
  • Managed Service for Apache Flink — stateful stream processing, SQL or Java, over your topics.
  • Lambda event source mapping — serverless consumers with batching and a failure destination.
  • Firehose & Glue — land topics in S3/Redshift/OpenSearch; schema registry for contracts.
Security, which is not optional
  • IAM authentication & authorization — the native AWS path; policies instead of ACLs.
  • SASL/SCRAM via Secrets Manager, or mTLS via ACM Private CA for existing clients.
  • TLS in transit, KMS encryption at rest, private VPC subnets — on by default or one setting away.
  • CloudWatch + Prometheus metrics; consumer lag is the metric to alarm on.
◆
Today's labs run on MSK Provisioned with Express brokers and IAM auth — that's why topic creation needs an explicit --replication-factor (unlike Serverless, which sets it for you). Every command works unchanged against Serverless, Confluent or self-managed Kafka; only the client config block differs.

Lab 1 Debrief / 04.3

What just happened.

Five minutes together before Lab 2. Each of these observations is a line from Chapter 02 that you've now verified yourself.

You wrote to a log

The producer appended to the end of one partition per record, chosen by the key's hash. No consumer had to exist — the data was durable regardless.

You held a cursor

--from-beginning only mattered the first time: after that your group had a committed offset, and Kafka resumed from it. The broker tracked one number, not three messages.

A new group saw history

Changing --group gave you the entire topic again, with no change to the producer and no effect on the first group. That's fan-out with zero coordination.

Choosing Well / 06.3

Pick for learning,
not for prestige.

The best first use case is rarely the most valuable one. It's the one that proves the capability end-to-end, in production, within a quarter — and leaves you with a team that knows how to run the next one.

A good first pilot looks like this
  • Two or three real consumers at launch — fan-out is the value you're demonstrating.
  • A producer you control, with an owner who will take the schema seriously.
  • Meaningful but not terrifying volume — enough to be real, small enough to survive mistakes.
  • Not on the critical path for money on day one — run it alongside the existing flow first.
  • A measurable before/after: latency, manual effort removed, data available to a team that didn't have it.
  • An exit in mind — what "it worked" means, and what happens next if it does.
Four pilots that reliably fail
  • "Replace all the MQ." A platform migration disguised as a pilot. No learning, maximum risk, political by week three.
  • A one-to-one handoff that genuinely has one consumer. You'll do more work for no new capability and conclude Kafka is overhead — correctly.
  • Long-running job dispatch. Per-message retry, visibility timeouts, priority. You'll rebuild SQS badly on top of a log.
  • A topic with no owner. "Publish everything and let teams figure it out" produces an unversioned payload nobody dares change and nobody trusts.
Not a fit at all — don't force it
  • Request/reply. You need a response to this call in 5 ms. Kafka can fake it with a correlation ID and a reply topic, but you've re-invented RPC with worse tooling. Use gRPC/HTTP.
  • Millions of topics. Per-tenant or per-user topics don't scale — each partition is files, memory and replication overhead. Model tenancy as keys inside a topic, not as topics.
  • Ad-hoc querying. There is no WHERE. If the access pattern is "find the record for user 42", you need a store — Kafka feeds it, it doesn't replace it.
  • Tiny scale, tiny team. Three services and 50 events/sec? A managed queue costs you a day, not a quarter. Kafka's floor is operational, not throughput.
▲
Hybrid is the normal end state. Streams for facts, queues for work, and a connector between them where a stream needs to trigger a job. Nobody in this room should leave planning to delete their queues.

The Big Idea / 08.1

The Superpower / 08.4

Durable + replayable
changes how you build.

Once history is retained, a whole class of hard operational problems turns into "reset an offset". This is the capability that most teams underuse for the first year.

Things replay makes cheap

Bug in a consumer

Fix the code, rewind the group to yesterday's offset, reprocess. No source system involvement, no export/import.

New consumer, old data

A brand-new service reads from earliest and bootstraps its own state from the same stream — no backfill job, no snapshot handoff.

Rebuild a derived store

Search index corrupted? Drop it, replay the topic, rebuild. The log is the source of truth; the index is a cache.

Audit & forensics

"What did we know at 14:32?" The exact bytes are still on disk with their offsets and timestamps.

Blue/green consumers

Run v2 of a processor in a second group against live traffic, compare outputs, then cut over.

Coordination / 09.2

KRaft: the cluster's
own metadata log.

Kafka's answer to "who is the leader of partition 7?" used to live in ZooKeeper — a separate system, with a separate ops story, and a push-based propagation model that got slower as clusters grew. KRaft stores metadata in a Kafka log replicated by Raft, so the cluster manages its own state with its own primitives.

__cluster_metadata — controller quorum
How it works
  • 3 or 5 nodes run in controller role. They elect an active controller via Raft; the others are hot standbys that already have the full log.
  • All metadata — topics, partitions, leaders, ISR, configs, ACLs — is written as records to a single-partition internal log, __cluster_metadata.
  • Brokers fetch that log like any consumer and replay it into an in-memory image. Metadata propagation becomes ordinary replication.
  • Each broker caches its own offset in that log, so a restart is a tail, not a full re-read.
✓
Failover drops from seconds/minutes (ZK: reload all state) to sub-second — the standby is already caught up.
✓
One system to run: one security model, one config style, one set of metrics. ZooKeeper removal completed in Kafka 4.0.
▲
Ops notes: controllers can be dedicated (recommended in production) or combined with broker role (fine for dev). Migration from ZK is a defined, staged procedure — and one-way. Quorum size is about failure tolerance, not throughput: 3 tolerates 1 loss, 5 tolerates 2.

Client Discovery / 09.5

How a client finds
the right broker.

There is no proxy and no router in front of Kafka. Clients are thick: they learn the cluster's layout and then talk directly to each partition's leader. Understanding this makes an entire family of connectivity bugs obvious.

The handshake
1 · BOOTSTRAPbootstrap.servers is only a seed list — any reachable broker will do. It is not a load balancer and not a permanent binding.
2 · METADATAThe client sends a Metadata request and gets back every broker's advertised host:port, plus the leader and ISR for each partition it cares about.
3 · DIRECT CONNECTThe client opens connections to the leaders and sends produce/fetch there. Writing to a non-leader is an error, not a redirect.
4 · REFRESHMetadata is refreshed every metadata.max.age.ms (5 min) and immediately on NOT_LEADER_OR_FOLLOWER — that error is normal during failover and is retried transparently.
✓
Consequence worth stating out loud: the client does the routing and the partitioning. Kafka's server stays simple because the smarts live in the library.
The bug everyone hits once
# server.properties — the classic mistake
listeners=PLAINTEXT://0.0.0.0:9092
# advertised.listeners not set → defaults to the
# broker's own hostname, e.g. "kafka-1" (container id)

# client, from outside Docker/k8s:

The Sizing Decision / 10.2

How many partitions?

The starting formula is simple; the interesting part is why both over- and under-provisioning hurt, and why the mistake is asymmetric.

Start with the math
# t = target throughput
# p = what ONE partition can produce at
# c = what ONE consumer instance can consume at

partitions = max( t/p , t/c )

# worked example
target        = 600 MB/s
per-partition = ~30 MB/s   → 20
per-consumer  = ~15 MB/s   → 40   ← binding
→ 40 partitions, then round up for headroom: 48

# then sanity-check the other constraints:
#  · ≥ max consumers you ever want in one group
#  · keys distribute evenly across 48 buckets?
#  · 48 × RF 3 = 144 replicas added to the cluster
▲
The consumer side is usually the binding constraint, because per-message processing (a DB write, an API call) is far slower than Kafka's own IO. Size for the slowest link in the chain, not for the broker.
Why the mistake is asymmetric

Too few

  • Consumer parallelism is capped — extra instances sit idle no matter how much lag you have.
  • Fixing it means adding partitions, which changes key→partition mapping for all future records.
  • You cannot reduce partition count at all. Ever. Only recreate the topic.

Too many

  • Each replica is open file handles + memory on the broker, and an entry in every follower's fetch loop.
  • Producer memory grows: a batch buffer per partition (batch.size each) — 10k partitions × 16 KiB is real RAM.
  • End-to-end latency rises: fewer records per batch per partition means less amortization.
  • Rebalances, leader elections and controller failovers all scale with partition count.
✓
Practical default: a small multiple of your broker count (6, 12, 24) for ordinary topics; size explicitly only for topics you have measured. Uniform is easier to operate than clever.

Distribution / 10.3

Keys, hashing,
and hot partitions.

The key does two jobs at once: it buys you per-entity ordering, and it decides load distribution. When those two goals conflict, you get a hot partition — one consumer at 100% while five idle.

key distribution — balanced vs skewed
How routing actually works
  • Key present: murmur2(keyBytes) % numPartitions — deterministic, language-independent, and identical across all official clients. Same key → same partition, forever (for a fixed partition count).
  • Key null: the modern default partitioner is sticky — it fills one partition's batch, sends it, then switches. That's better than round-robin because it produces fuller batches and therefore lower latency and higher throughput.
  • Explicit partition: you can pass one to send() and bypass all of it. Useful, and easy to regret.
Fixing skew

Composite key

tenant:bucket where bucket = hash(orderId)%16. Spreads the whale, keeps ordering within a bucket. You must be able to live with that weaker guarantee.

Separate topic

Route the top-N tenants to their own topic with its own partition count and consumer group. Isolation beats cleverness.

Two-stage

Ingest unkeyed for even spread, then re-key into a second topic once the heavy work is done.

Custom partitioner

Implement Partitioner for known hot keys. Remember: consumers don't know your scheme, so document it or it becomes tribal knowledge.

What this explains
  • Groups vanish after a while. offsets.retention.minutes (7 days by default) expires the offsets of an inactive group. Restart a weekly job after a long break and it resets per auto.offset.reset — a classic Monday-morning duplicate storm.

Performance / 11.5

Why a JVM app on
disks beats your cache.

Kafka's throughput is not the result of clever code; it's the result of four decisions that let it do almost nothing per message.

01Sequential IO

Appending to the tail of a file is the one access pattern every storage device is good at — hundreds of MB/s even on spinning rust, no seeks, no b-tree rebalancing, no write amplification.

02Page cache, not heap

Writes go to the OS page cache; the kernel flushes them. Reads of recent data never touch disk. No GC pressure, and cache survives a broker process restart because it lives in the kernel.

03Zero copy

sendfile() moves bytes from page cache straight to the NIC — no copy into user space, no JVM objects, no serialization on the read path. The broker is mostly a byte pipe.

04Batching everywhere

Producer batches, broker appends batches, consumer fetches batches, followers replicate batches. Every per-request cost is amortized over hundreds of records.

Things that silently disable the fast path
✕
TLS defeats zero-copy: bytes must be encrypted, so they pass through user space. Expect meaningful CPU cost — budget for it rather than discovering it in prod.
✕
Broker-side recompression (timestamp rewriting, old client formats) turns a byte pipe into a CPU-bound transcoder.
✕
Lagging consumers read cold data from disk instead of cache — one badly behaved backfill can push p99 latency up for everyone on the broker.
✕
Tiny records, no linger. linger.ms=0 with 200-byte records means one request per record. Setting linger.ms=5 is often a 10× throughput change for ~5 ms of latency.
Durability ≠ fsync

Kafka does not fsync every write. log.flush.interval.messages is effectively unset by default and the kernel decides when pages hit the platter.

Durability instead comes from replication: acks=all + min.insync.replicas=2 means the data is in the page cache of multiple machines, ideally in different failure domains. Two independent machines losing power in the same second is the risk you're accepting — and it's a better trade than fsync-per-write on every node.

This is worth stating explicitly to any audience with a database background — it is the single most surprising design choice in Kafka, and it's the reason it is fast.

No Duplicates / 12.4

The idempotent producer.

Without it, a retry after a lost acknowledgement (not a lost write) creates a duplicate — the write succeeded, the response didn't arrive. Idempotence lets the broker recognise the retry and discard it. It's on by default in modern clients, and it's nearly free.

PID + epoch + sequence — duplicate rejected
The mechanism
  • On init the producer gets a producer id (PID) and an epoch from the broker.
  • Every batch carries a monotonic sequence number per partition.
  • Each leader remembers the last sequence it accepted per (PID, partition), for the last 5 batches.
  • Sequence = expected → append. Already seen → return success, write nothing. Too far ahead → OutOfOrderSequenceException, because a batch was permanently lost and silently accepting would break ordering.
enable.idempotence=true      default since 3.0
# implies, and validates:
acks=all
retries>0                    effectively MAX_INT
max.in.flight.requests.per.connection <= 5
✓
The subtle win: before idempotence, retries with more than one in-flight request could reorder records (batch 2 lands, batch 1 retries and lands after). With sequence numbers the broker enforces order, so you keep pipelining and ordering — you no longer have to set max.in.flight=1 and destroy throughput.
▲
Scope: this dedups the producer's own retries, within a partition. It does not dedup your application calling send() twice for the same business event — that's your idempotency key, not Kafka's.

Atomicity / 12.5

Transactions across
partitions and topics.

Idempotence fixes duplicates within one partition. Transactions make a set of writes — across partitions and topics, plus the consumer's own offset commit — atomically visible or not visible at all. This is the machinery behind "exactly-once" in stream processing.

consume → transform → produce, atomically
producer.initTransactions();   // fences old epochs

while (true) {
  records = consumer.poll(...);
  producer.beginTransaction();
  try {
    for (r : records) {
      producer.send("enriched", transform(r));
      producer.send("audit",    audit(r));
    }
    // the offsets travel INSIDE the transaction
    producer.sendOffsetsToTransaction(
        offsetsOf(records), consumer.groupMetadata());
    producer.commitTransaction();
  } catch (Exception e) {
    producer.abortTransaction();   // nothing visible
  }
}

# producer side
transactional.id=enricher-7   stable, per instance
# consumer side — mandatory, or you see aborted data
isolation.level=read_committed
What the cluster does
  • A transaction coordinator (a broker, chosen by hashing transactional.id) owns the transaction and logs its state to __transaction_state.
  • transactional.id maps to a PID + epoch. A new instance with the same id bumps the epoch and fences the old one — that's how a zombie after a crash can't commit.
  • On commit, the coordinator writes control records (COMMIT/ABORT markers) into every partition the transaction touched. They occupy offsets but are invisible to your application.
  • read_committed consumers buffer records past the LSO and filter aborted ones using those markers, so they only ever hand you committed data.
✕
Cost: extra round trips + markers per transaction. Very small transactions are expensive — batch a poll's worth of work, not one record. Expect a real throughput hit versus plain idempotent produce.
✕
A hung transaction blocks readers. read_committed can't advance past the LSO, so an instance that dies mid-transaction stalls consumers until transaction.timeout.ms (60s default) aborts it.
◆
In Kafka Streams this is one line: processing.guarantee=exactly_once_v2. If you're doing Kafka-to-Kafka processing, use Streams (or Flink) rather than hand-rolling the loop on the left.

  • UnknownTopicOrPartitionException — often just stale metadata during a failover; also what you see when auto-create is off and the topic really doesn't exist.
  • Callback never fires — you're blocking inside another callback. The sender thread is single; blocking it stops the whole producer.

Client Internals / 13.2

The poll loop is
the whole API.

poll() does far more than return records: it joins the group, sends fetches, drives the assignment protocol, and — historically — kept your membership alive. Most consumer bugs are poll-loop bugs.

consumer.subscribe(List.of("orders"));

while (running) {
  // ONE call: joins group, heartbeats liveness,
  // fetches, and advances the position
  records = consumer.poll(Duration.ofMillis(500));

  for (r : records) {
    process(r);            // ← your latency lives here
  }
  consumer.commitSync();  // after processing = at-least-once
}
consumer.close();          // sends LeaveGroup →
                           // fast rebalance instead of a 45s timeout
✕
The classic outage: a batch takes longer than max.poll.interval.ms (5 min). The coordinator assumes the consumer is dead, revokes its partitions, rebalances — then your commit fails with CommitFailedException, the work is redone elsewhere, and if processing is always slow you get a permanent rebalance loop where nothing ever completes.
✓

Membership / 13.3

How the group
agrees on who reads what.

There is no scheduler assigning partitions. The group elects one member as leader, and that client computes the assignment — the broker only relays it. Knowing this makes rebalance logs readable.

join → sync → steady state
The exchange
1 · FIND COORDINATORClient hashes the group id to a partition of __consumer_offsets; that partition's leader is the group coordinator.
2 · JOIN GROUPEvery member sends its subscriptions and supported assignors. The coordinator holds them all until the group settles, then names the first joiner as group leader and bumps the generation id.
3 · SYNC GROUPThe leader (a client!) runs the assignor and sends the full assignment to the coordinator, which fans out each member's slice in its SyncGroup response.
4 · STEADY STATEMembers fetch from their partitions and heartbeat. Any membership or metadata change → new generation, repeat.
◆
The generation id fences stragglers: a commit stamped with an old generation is rejected. That's what CommitFailedException after a rebalance is telling you — "you no longer own this partition".
▲
Rebalance triggers, in order of how often they surprise people: a member joins/leaves/crashes · max.poll.interval exceeded · partition count of a subscribed topic changes · a subscription regex starts matching a new topic.

The Painful Part / 13.4

Rebalancing: four
generations of fixes.

The original protocol stopped the world. Every improvement since has chipped away at that. Knowing which one you're running determines whether a deploy costs you 50 ms or 30 seconds of stalled consumption.

V1Eager

Stop the world. Every member revokes all partitions, then the group re-assigns from scratch. Nobody consumes during the rebalance.

One pod restart in a 50-pod group pauses all 50.

V2Cooperative
incremental

KIP-429. Members keep partitions they'll retain; only moved partitions are revoked, in a second short rebalance.

partition.assignment.strategy=
CooperativeStickyAssignor

V3Static
membership

KIP-345. Give each instance a stable group.instance.id. A restart within session.timeout.ms reclaims the same partitions with no rebalance at all.

Ideal for StatefulSets and rolling deploys.

V4KIP-848

The new consumer group protocol: assignment moves to the broker, driven incrementally, and the stop-the-world barrier disappears entirely.

group.protocol=consumer — the default direction of travel in Kafka 4.x.

Why it hurts more than it looks
  • Stateful consumers pay twice. Losing a partition means discarding local state (a window, a cache, a Streams store) and rebuilding it after reassignment — seconds to minutes, not milliseconds.
  • Duplicates. Uncommitted records at revocation are re-delivered to the new owner. Rebalances are the biggest single source of duplicate processing.
  • Cascades. Post-rebalance load spikes trip max.poll.interval on other members, which triggers the next rebalance. Loops are self-sustaining.
  • Deploys multiply it. A naive rolling restart of N pods triggers up to 2N rebalances.
Practical mitigations, in order
  • Use cooperative sticky (or the new protocol) — one config line, immediate win.
  • Add group.instance.id for anything stateful or frequently redeployed.
  • Implement ConsumerRebalanceListener: commit in onPartitionsRevoked, and warm/flush state in the callbacks.
  • Always close() on shutdown — a clean LeaveGroup avoids a 45-second timeout wait.
  • Keep max.poll.records small enough that one poll can never exceed the interval.
  • Alert on rebalance rate, not just lag. A healthy group rebalances on deploys — and otherwise, never.

Assignment Strategy / 13.5

Who gets which partition.

The assignor runs inside the group leader's client, which means it's pluggable and you can reason about it. The differences look academic until you have state or uneven topics.

Range

Per topic, hand out contiguous ranges of partitions. Simple — and systematically unfair: with 2 topics of 3 partitions and 2 consumers, consumer A gets 4 and B gets 2, because the remainder lands on the same consumer for every topic.

The old default. Rarely what you want.

Round robin

Lay every topic-partition in one list and deal them out. Fair distribution — but it reshuffles everything on every membership change, so all state is invalidated.

Sticky

Fair and minimally disruptive: keeps existing assignments wherever possible and only moves what balance requires. Fewer partitions move → less state rebuilt.

Cooperative sticky

Sticky's placement plus the incremental rebalance protocol. The default choice today for anything you care about.

Notes from the field
  • All members must agree on a strategy — during a migration, list both old and new so the group can roll: partition.assignment.strategy=CooperativeSticky,Range, deploy, then drop the old one. Skipping the two-step means a group that can't form.
  • Custom assignors are legitimate. Rack-aware placement (keep consumers in the same AZ as their leader), weighted assignment for uneven partitions, or co-partitioning two topics so joins stay local.
  • Kafka Streams ships its own assignor — it co-partitions inputs and places standby replicas for state stores. Don't override it.
Assign vs subscribe
// subscribe: dynamic, in a group, rebalances,

// assign: manual, NO group coordination,
// NO rebalancing, NO automatic assignment

    new TopicPartition("orders", 3)));

Mechanics / 14.2

Followers are
just consumers.

There is no separate replication protocol. A follower issues Fetch requests to the leader exactly like a consumer does — same code path, same batching, same zero-copy. Elegant, and it means the high watermark advances as a side effect of fetching.

follower fetch → HW advance
The loop
1 · FETCHFollower sends Fetch(partition, offset=its own LEO). That offset implicitly acknowledges everything before it — no separate ack message exists.
2 · APPENDFollower appends the returned batches to its own log, advancing its LEO.
3 · HW ADVANCEThe leader sets HW = min(LEO of all ISR members). Only now are those records visible to consumers.
4 · PROPAGATEThe new HW rides along in the next fetch response — which is why followers learn it one round trip late, and why a follower's view of the HW always lags slightly.
◆
Leader epoch (KIP-101) is the fix for a real historical bug class: after a failover, replicas could truncate to the wrong point and diverge. Each leadership term gets a monotonic epoch number; recovering replicas ask "where did epoch N end?" and truncate precisely. It's why leader-epoch-checkpoint is on disk and why the epoch is in every batch header.
▲
Tuning: num.replica.fetchers (default 1) is a common bottleneck on brokers with many partitions — replication is single-threaded per source broker by default. Raise it when RemoteTimeMs or under-replicated counts climb during load.

The ISR / 14.3

Shrink, expand,
and what it costs you.

The ISR is dynamic membership, and it is the single most informative thing to watch on a Kafka cluster. Every durability guarantee is phrased in terms of it.

The rules
  • A replica is in sync if it has fetched up to the leader's log-end offset within replica.lag.time.max.ms (30 s default). Note it's time-based, not message-count based — a modern fix, because a lag-in-messages threshold falsely ejects replicas during traffic spikes.
  • Fall behind → the leader shrinks the ISR and records it in cluster metadata. Catch up → expand.
  • acks=all means "all current ISR members", so a shrinking ISR silently weakens your guarantee — which is exactly why min.insync.replicas exists as a floor.
  • Only ISR members are eligible for leadership (unless you allow unclean election).
Why replicas fall out
  • GC pause on the follower — a 40-second stop-the-world beats the 30-second threshold. The reason big heaps are discouraged.
  • Slow or saturated disk on one broker — often one bad EBS volume.
  • Network saturation, particularly cross-AZ during a backfill.
  • Not enough fetcher threads for the partition count.
  • A genuine traffic spike the follower can't keep up with.

Walkthroughs / 14.4

Three failures,
step by step.

Rehearse these out loud. Being able to narrate a failover second by second is what separates "we use Kafka" from "we operate Kafka".

Scenario A · clean

Leader broker dies

t+0Broker 1 crashes. Producers to its partitions start timing out.
t+~1sController notices the session loss and picks a new leader from the ISR for every partition broker 1 led.
t+~1sMetadata update propagates. Clients get NOT_LEADER_OR_FOLLOWER, refresh metadata, and retry.
afterISR is now 2 of 3. With min.insync=2 you're still writable. UnderReplicated > 0 until broker 1 returns or you reassign.

Data loss: none. The new leader had everything up to the HW, and only HW-committed data was ever visible.

Scenario B · unclean

All ISR members die

t+0Leader and both in-sync followers are gone. Only a stale out-of-sync replica remains.
choiceunclean.leader.election=false (default): the partition goes offline. No reads, no writes, until an ISR member returns.
choice=true: the stale replica becomes leader. The cluster is available again — and every record it never received is permanently gone, including acknowledged ones.
worseConsumers that already read those offsets now see different data at the same offsets. The log has silently forked.

This is the CAP trade, made explicit as a config flag. Keep it false unless you can articulate why losing data is preferable.

Scenario C · sneaky

The slow broker

t+0One broker's disk degrades. It doesn't die — it just gets slow.
effectAs a follower it flaps in and out of ISR. As a leader it makes acks=all produce latency spike for every partition it leads.
effectPurgatory fills; p99 produce latency climbs cluster-wide because clients batch multiple partitions per request.
fixMove leadership off it, then reassign its replicas away, then replace the host. A degraded broker is worse than a dead one — failure detection only handles the dead case.

Watch IsrShrinksPerSec per broker and per-broker p99 produce latency to catch this early.

◆
Rehearse it for real. Kill a broker in staging during load and time the recovery. The number you measure — usually 1–3 seconds of elevated errors with retrying clients — is the number to quote when someone asks "what happens if a node dies?"

Topology & DR / 14.5

Across zones,
across regions.

Replication within a cluster gives you broker fault tolerance. Surviving a zone or region needs deliberate placement — and the bill for it is mostly network.

Rack awareness

Set broker.rack to the AZ. Kafka then spreads each partition's replicas across racks automatically, so RF=3 over 3 AZs survives losing a whole zone with min.insync=2 intact.

Cost: every write crosses AZ boundaries (RF−1) times, and inter-AZ transfer is usually billed.

Follower fetching

KIP-392: with client.rack on the consumer and a rack-aware replica selector on the broker, consumers read from a local follower instead of a possibly-remote leader.

Win: big reduction in cross-AZ read traffic and latency. Caveat: followers serve only up to their view of the HW, so add a little latency.

Stretch cluster

One cluster spanning 3 AZs (or nearby regions) with rack-aware placement. Synchronous — no failover step, no offset translation, RPO zero.

Requires low, stable inter-AZ latency: every acks=all write pays it. Across distant regions, don't.

MirrorMaker 2

Asynchronous replication between clusters (Connect-based). Mirrors topics, configs, ACLs and translates consumer offsets between clusters.

Reality: non-zero RPO, and failover is an operational procedure — offsets don't line up exactly. Test it, don't assume it.

Active/active

Two regions, each with local producers, mirroring each other with topic prefixes to avoid loops. Great availability; you now own conflict resolution at the application level.

Tiered storage

Old segments live in object storage, which is already multi-AZ durable. Shrinks local disk, makes rebalancing fast, and lets retention stretch to months — but historical reads get object-store latency.

▲
Say the quiet part: for a high-throughput cluster, cross-AZ replication traffic can be the largest line item — larger than the instances. Compression, follower fetching and honest retention are cost levers, not just performance ones.
◆
Define RPO/RTO before choosing. "Zero data loss" → stretch cluster and accept the latency. "Minutes of loss acceptable" → MM2 and accept the failover runbook. There is no option that gives you both cheaply.

Ordering / 15.2

What actually
guarantees order.

The guarantee is narrow and precise: records with the same key, produced by one producer to one partition, are consumed in the order they were produced. Every clause in that sentence is load-bearing.

Five ways it breaks
  • Different partitions. No cross-partition ordering exists — not even approximately. Two consumers process partition 0 and 1 at unrelated speeds.
  • Retries without idempotence. With max.in.flight>1 and enable.idempotence=false, batch 2 can land before a retried batch 1. Silent reordering.
  • Partition count change. After a resize, a key's records live in two partitions with no ordering between them.
  • Rebalances + concurrency. Hand records to a thread pool and you've discarded partition order inside your own app, whatever Kafka delivered.
  • Multiple producers, same key. Kafka orders by arrival at the leader. Two producers writing the same entity race, and the winner is whoever's packet arrives first — not whoever acted first.
✕
The last one is the deepest. If two services can write the same entity, Kafka's ordering won't save you — you need a single writer per key, or version numbers / optimistic concurrency in the payload.
Getting ordering you can rely on
  • Key by the entity whose order matters — accountId, not eventId. This is the single most consequential modelling decision on the producer side.
  • One writer per key. Route commands for an entity through one owner service (or one Streams task).
  • Keep enable.idempotence=true (the default) so pipelining and ordering coexist.
  • Process a partition in one thread, or use a key-affine executor that pins a key to a worker.
  • Carry a version or timestamp in the payload so downstream can reject stale updates even if ordering is violated. Defence in depth.
✓
Reframe for the audience: ordering isn't something Kafka gives you — it's something you buy with a key, and pay for with a limit on parallelism for that key. That trade is the whole partition model in one sentence.

Honest EOS / 15.3

Where exactly-once
stops being true.

Kafka's exactly-once is real, but it is scoped: reads and writes within Kafka. The moment you touch the outside world, the guarantee becomes your responsibility.

Holds

Kafka → Kafka

Read from topic A, transform, write to B and C, commit offsets — all in one transaction, consumed with read_committed.

This genuinely works. Kafka Streams with exactly_once_v2, or Flink with its Kafka sink, gives it to you as a config value.

Needs your help

Kafka → database

The transaction can't span Kafka and Postgres. Two options that actually work:

1. Store the offset in the same DB transaction as the data, and seek() to it on assignment. Now "processed" and "recorded" commit together.

2. Make the write idempotent — upsert by a business key, or INSERT ... ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING on a dedup table keyed by (topic,partition,offset).

Cannot hold

Kafka → external side effect

Charging a card, sending an email, calling a third-party API. You cannot make "process the record" and "the side effect happened" atomic across a network you don't control.

The only real answer is an idempotency key that the receiving system honours (every serious payments API supports one). Kafka's job is to guarantee you get the record at least once; deduplication belongs at the boundary.

◆
Useful framing for skeptics: "exactly-once" doesn't mean each record is physically delivered once — retries still happen. It means the observable effect occurs once. That's why idempotence at the boundary is always part of the design, never an admission of failure.
✓
Design rule: make handlers idempotent first, then decide whether transactions are still worth the cost. Teams that do it the other way round end up with slow pipelines that still need idempotent handlers.

You rarely use
Kafka alone.

Most of the value people attribute to "Kafka" is actually delivered by the layer around it. Knowing which tool owns which job saves a lot of custom code.

Kafka Connect

A runtime for source and sink connectors — Debezium for database CDC, S3/JDBC/Elastic/Snowflake sinks. Distributed, with offset and config state kept in Kafka topics.

Rule: if you're writing a service whose only job is to copy data between Kafka and a store, a connector probably exists.

Kafka Streams

A library (no cluster) for joins, windowed aggregations, and local state backed by RocksDB with a compacted changelog topic. Scales by adding instances; exactly-once with one setting.

Best fit when you want stream processing inside an existing JVM service.

Apache Flink

A cluster for heavier stream processing: event-time semantics, large state, SQL, and multi-source pipelines. The default choice for large or long-lived streaming jobs.

Streams for embedded and simple; Flink for big, stateful and operationally owned.

Schema Registry

Kafka values are opaque bytes — nothing stops a producer changing the shape and breaking every consumer. A registry stores Avro/Protobuf/JSON-Schema versions and enforces compatibility at serialization time.

Adopt this early. Retrofitting schemas onto a live topic is genuinely painful.

Tiered storage

Old segments offloaded to object storage (KIP-405). Retention measured in months without giant brokers, and much faster rebalances because less data is local.

Where it's heading

KRaft everywhere (ZooKeeper removed in 4.0) · KIP-848 broker-side rebalancing · share groups / queue semantics (KIP-932) bringing per-message acknowledgement to Kafka · diskless / object-store-first designs aimed squarely at cross-AZ cost.

▲
Governance is the part that bites at scale. Naming conventions, topic ownership, schema compatibility rules, retention defaults, and an ACL model — decide these while you have 20 topics, not 2,000. The technology scales more gracefully than the conventions do.

Day two: the dashboard
and the habits.

If you take one operational artifact from this talk, take this list. Four pages, a handful of alerts, three habits.

The dashboard, in four panels
PanelMetrics
Cluster healthActiveControllerCount (=1) · OfflinePartitionsCount · UnderReplicatedPartitions · UnderMinIsrPartitionCount
Broker loadBytes in/out per broker · request rate · RequestQueueTimeMs, LocalTimeMs, RemoteTimeMs p99 · idle % of network and IO threads · disk used
Producersrecord-error-rate · request-latency p99 · buffer-available-bytes · batch-size-avg · records-per-request (batching health)
ConsumersLag per group per partition · records-lag-max · commit rate + failures · rebalance rate · fetch latency
Alert on these; graph the rest
  • UnderMinIsrPartitionCount > 0 → writes are being rejected now
  • OfflinePartitionsCount > 0 → data unavailable now
  • ActiveControllerCount != 1 across the cluster
  • Consumer lag rising for N minutes (per group, not aggregate)
  • Disk > 75% on any broker — retention math needs revisiting before it's 95%
  • Rebalance rate above baseline outside deploy windows
Habits that prevent incidents
  • Never auto-create topics in production. auto.create.topics.enable=false. A typo in a producer shouldn't create a 1-partition, RF-1 topic that quietly becomes load-bearing.
  • Set explicit defaults for RF, min.insync.replicas and retention at the cluster level so the lazy path is the safe path.
  • Rolling restarts, one broker at a time, waiting for UnderReplicatedPartitions=0 between each. Never two at once with RF=3.
  • Throttle every reassignment. Unthrottled rebalances have taken down more clusters than hardware has.
  • Own client versions. Old clients force broker-side format conversion; very old ones lack idempotence by default.
  • Practise a broker kill and a group offset reset in staging, with the runbook open, before you need either.
  • Quotas (producer_byte_rate, consumer_byte_rate, request percentage) on a multi-tenant cluster — one team's backfill shouldn't be everyone's incident.

Close / 16.4

Ten sentences that
carry the whole talk.

  • 1. Kafka is a durable, replayable, append-only log — storage that speaks pub/sub, not a queue that forgets.
  • 2. The partition is the unit of ordering, parallelism and storage. A topic itself has no order.
  • 3. The key buys per-entity ordering and decides load distribution — pick it deliberately, and expect skew.
  • 4. Records are stored in batches, in segment files, found via sparse indexes; performance comes from sequential IO, the page cache and zero-copy.
  • 5. Durability comes from replication, not fsync: acks=all plus min.insync.replicas=2.
  • 6. Consumers pull and own their offsets, which are themselves records in a compacted topic.
  • 7. Rebalancing is where most production pain lives — cooperative sticky, static membership, small polls.
  • 8. The high watermark is why replication lag shows up as consumer latency.
  • 9. At-least-once with idempotent handlers is the right default; exactly-once is real but scoped to Kafka→Kafka.
  • 10. Kafka is boringly reliable when you respect four numbers: RF, min.insync.replicas, partition count, and retention.

Producers append. Brokers replicate. Consumers replay.  — that's Kafka.
